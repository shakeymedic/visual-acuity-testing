Why it matters: acuity is the "vital sign" of the eye

Every eye presentation

Record visual acuity in every patient with an eye complaint or facial or orbital trauma, including when the complaint seems minor.

A baseline for later comparison

Later clinicians compare against your number. A deterioration can only be detected if an accurate starting value exists.

Referral and medico-legal record

Ophthalmology will ask for it when you refer. A missing acuity is a common gap in eye-related complaints and claims.

Correct distance

6 m for a standard Snellen chart, or 3 m to a mirror with a reversed chart (the light path is still 6 m). A chart made for 3 m is read at 3 m.

Distance glasses ON

Test with the patient's usual distance glasses or contact lenses. Reading glasses blur distance vision, so take them off.

One eye at a time

Cover the other eye with an occluder, or with the palm of the hand. Do not use gaps between the fingers, and do not press on the eye.

Test acuity before instilling drops (anaesthetic, fluorescein, dilating), before examining with bright lights, and before any procedure. Drops and bright lights can temporarily reduce the reading.

Exception: chemical injury. Start irrigation immediately. Do not delay it to measure acuity. Check acuity once irrigation is under way or completed, and record when it was measured.

Make sure the chart is evenly lit and free of glare, and that the patient sits or stands at the marked position.

Ask about baseline vision: a known "lazy eye" (amblyopia), previous eye disease or surgery, and when their glasses were last updated. A reduced reading may be longstanding.

1 The procedure (distance acuity)

  1. R Right eye first (a fixed routine avoids R/L mix-ups). Cover the left eye. Ask the patient to read from the top line down.
  2. Push to the limit. Encourage them to guess until they get most of a line wrong. Squinting or leaning forward gives a falsely good result, so ask them not to.
  3. Record the smallest line on which more than half the letters were read correctly. Add letters missed on that line ("−") or letters read on the next line down ("+"). See the worked example.
  4. L Repeat for the left eye. Patients can memorise letters, so ask them to read the lines in reverse or use a different chart or line if you can.
  5. Pinhole any eye that reads worse than 6/6 (see below).

Worked example: partial lines

This example assumes 5 letters per line. Snellen charts have different numbers of letters on each line, so count what is on your chart.

6/12 D ✓  F ✓  N ✓  P ✓  T ✓
6/9 H ✓  Z ✓  V ✓  C ✗  O ✗
6/6 all ✗
3 of 5 letters on the 6/9 line (more than half) → record 6/9 -2.
If only 2 of 5 had been read on the 6/9 line → record 6/12 +2.

Departments differ slightly in how they record partial lines. What matters most is that the notation is clear and used consistently.

2 The pinhole test

When to use

Any eye reading worse than 6/6

Some departments use 6/9 or 6/12 as the cut-off, so follow local policy.

The patient keeps their glasses on, holds the pinhole occluder over the eye being tested and reads the chart again. The pinhole only lets through light that does not need focusing, so it largely bypasses refractive error.

IMPROVES

Refractive element likely

Often needs updated glasses. Improvement does not rule out other disease. A new refractive change can itself be a sign of cataract or high blood glucose, for example.

NO IMPROVEMENT

Look for another cause

Possible causes: cloudy media (cornea, lens or vitreous), retina or macula, optic nerve, amblyopia, or a problem in the brain's visual pathways. The eye needs examining.

Some patients find the pinhole hard to use (for example children, or patients who cannot find the hole), which gives a false "no improvement". In macular disease or dense cataract, the pinhole can make vision worse.

3 Near vision (when relevant)

Use a reading test card with N-point print sizes (N5, N6, N8, N10 and so on; a smaller number means smaller print). Test each eye with the patient's reading glasses on, at their usual reading distance (often around 33–40 cm). Record the smallest print read and the distance used, e.g. Near: R N6, L N8 at 35 cm c reading gl. Near vision is useful when the patient cannot get to a distance chart, but it does not replace distance acuity.

Low vision ladder

Use when the top letter (6/60) cannot be read at 6 m. Go down one step at a time.

  1. 1

    Move closer

    Walk the patient towards the chart (3 m, 2 m, 1 m). The top line read at 3 m = 3/60, at 1 m = 1/60.

    3/60 … 1/60

  2. 2

    Counting fingers

    Hold up fingers against a plain background, starting at 1 m and moving closer. Record the distance.

    CF @ 1 m

  3. 3

    Hand movements

    Hold your hand still, or move it slowly, and ask "is it moving?". Record the distance.

    HM

  4. 4

    Perception of light

    Shine a bright pen torch into the eye, switching it on and off, with the other eye firmly covered. If light is seen, ask which direction it comes from (projection).

    PL

  5. 5

    No perception of light

    No light seen with a bright torch. Confirm this carefully: it is a very serious finding.

    NPL

You may also see "LP" and "NLP" in place of PL and NPL. Both are in use; follow your department's convention.

Interpreting visual acuity

Reading the Snellen fraction

6
18

Top number: the distance (metres) at which the patient read the chart.

Bottom number: the distance at which a person with standard vision could read that line.

So 6/18 means that what this patient can just read at 6 m, a person with standard vision could read from 18 m away. A bigger bottom number means worse vision.

What counts as "normal"?

  • 6/6 is the standard reference point. Many healthy young adults read 6/5 or better, so 6/6 is not the best possible result.
  • Acuity tends to decline with age. In an older adult, a reduced reading may be longstanding. Ask about it and compare with previous records.
  • A difference between the two eyes, or a drop from the patient's known baseline, is often more informative than the absolute number.
  • Snellen testing varies between tests. A difference of a single line may be noise. Take a difference of two or more lines (or new symptoms with any drop) seriously.

logMAR in brief

  • logMAR charts have the same number of letters on every line and equal steps in size between lines. This makes them more precise and they are standard in eye clinics and research.
  • 0.00 = 6/6. Higher numbers mean worse vision. Negative numbers mean better than 6/6.
  • Each line is 0.1, and on a standard 5-letter line each letter is worth 0.02.
  • Scoring: take the smallest line read completely and subtract 0.02 for each extra letter read on smaller lines. For example, all of 0.20 plus 3 letters of 0.10 gives 0.20 − 0.06 = 0.14.

Putting it together: reduced acuity in the ED

Sudden & painless
  • Retinal artery occlusion (time-critical)
  • Retinal vein occlusion
  • Retinal detachment (flashes, floaters, curtain)
  • Vitreous haemorrhage
  • Ischaemic optic neuropathy, including giant cell arteritis
  • Stroke or TIA (think visual field loss, often in both eyes)
Sudden & painful / red
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma
  • Microbial keratitis (especially contact lens wearers)
  • Anterior uveitis
  • Optic neuritis (pain on eye movement)
  • Endophthalmitis (after recent surgery or injection)
  • Trauma, chemical injury
Gradual
  • Refractive error (improves with pinhole)
  • Cataract
  • Age-related macular degeneration
  • Diabetic eye disease
  • Chronic glaucoma (usually affects the visual field before acuity)

Gradual loss can still be important. Wet macular degeneration, for example, needs prompt referral.

These lists are examples to help your thinking, not a complete differential. Normal acuity does not exclude serious disease: glaucoma, papilloedema and many visual field defects can all occur with 6/6 vision.

Useful reference point: the DVLA driving standard

For car and motorcycle drivers (Group 1), the DVLA requires acuity of at least decimal 0.5 (6/12) using both eyes together (or in the only eye), with glasses or contact lenses if needed. Drivers must also be able to read a number plate at 20 metres and meet a visual field standard. Group 2 (lorry and bus) standards are stricter.

Check the current DVLA guidance before advising a patient: standards can change. If you think a patient may not meet the standard, advise them not to drive and to tell the DVLA, and document that advice.

Documenting visual acuity

Every acuity entry should say…

  • Which eye. Right and left recorded separately, written as R and L.
  • The result with partial-line notation (e.g. 6/9 -2), or the low-vision level with its distance (e.g. CF @ 50 cm).
  • Correction: unaided, own glasses, or contact lenses.
  • Pinhole result if acuity was reduced (a value, or "NI" for no improvement).
  • Chart and distance if not a standard 6 m Snellen chart (e.g. logMAR, 3 m chart, near card).
  • Timing: especially if measured after drops, after irrigation, or repeated to look for change.
  • If it could not be done, say why (e.g. "VA not obtainable – patient sedated; to be repeated"), and plan to repeat it.

Good examples, decoded

VA s gl: R 6/6, L 6/9 -1

Unaided. Right eye 6/6. Left eye read the 6/9 line but missed one letter.

VA c gl: R 6/24 PH 6/9, L 6/6

With own glasses. Right eye reduced, with a clear improvement using the pinhole, so a refractive element is likely. Still examine the eye.

VA c gl: R 6/6, L CF @ 1 m PH NI

Left eye can only count fingers at 1 m, with no pinhole improvement, so the cause is not refractive. Ask whether this is new or longstanding.

VA (logMAR, CL): R 0.00, L 0.14

logMAR chart, wearing contact lenses. Left eye is equivalent to roughly 6/8.

VA (after 2 L irrigation, 14:20) s gl: R 6/9, L 6/36 PH NI

Chemical injury. Acuity checked after irrigation, with the timing and context recorded.

Avoid these

  • "Vision normal" / "Vision grossly intact" Not measured and cannot be compared later. Record numbers.
  • "VA 6/6" Which eye? Both? With or without glasses?
  • "L CF" At what distance? Pinhole? What about the right eye?
  • "Can see" / "Able to read clock" Not a standard measurement. If no chart is available, use a near card and document it as such.
  • "6/6 PH" Unclear whether 6/6 was the result before or with the pinhole. Record both, e.g. "6/12 PH 6/6".

    Special situations

    Children

    Use a test suited to the child's age, such as crowded picture or letter-matching tests (e.g. Kay Pictures or Keeler crowded logMAR). In pre-verbal children, check whether they fix on and follow a target, and whether they object when one eye is covered. Expected acuity varies with age. Follow your local paediatric or orthoptic guidance and record the test used.

    Non-readers or language barriers

    Use a tumbling E, Landolt C or picture chart, where the patient points to show the direction or matches a shape. Use a professional interpreter. Record the chart used, e.g. "VA (tumbling E)".

    Patient cannot reach the chart

    A near card at the bedside is better than nothing. Record it clearly as near vision (e.g. "N8 at 35 cm"), not as a Snellen value, and repeat distance acuity when possible. Chart apps on phones exist, but check your department's policy before relying on one.

    Swollen lids / trauma

    Gently open the lids without pressing on the globe, especially if a penetrating injury is possible. Even "PL present" is valuable. Record why a fuller assessment was not possible.

    Reduced consciousness

    Formal acuity is not possible. Record "VA not obtainable" with the reason. Check and record pupils (including a relative afferent pupillary defect, RAPD, when this can be assessed). Repeat acuity when the patient is able.

    Forgotten glasses

    Test unaided, then with a pinhole: the pinhole gives an estimate of corrected acuity. Record it as "s gl … PH …" and note that the patient's glasses were not available.

    Test yourself

    Think of your answer, then tap to reveal.

    After acuity: the rest of the eye assessment

    1. External inspection

    Examine the orbit, eyelids, conjunctiva and sclera for redness, swelling or discharge. Evert the upper lid to look for a hidden foreign body, unless a penetrating injury is suspected. Look at the anterior chamber for blood (hyphaema) or pus (hypopyon).

    2. Pupils

    Note size, shape and symmetry. Test the direct and consensual light reflexes. Use the swinging light test to look for a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). With reduced acuity, an RAPD points towards retinal or optic nerve disease.

    3. Eye movements

    Ask the patient to follow a target in an "H" pattern. Ask about double vision, and about pain when the eyes move.

    4. Visual fields

    Test by confrontation, sitting level with the patient. Test each eye separately, in all four quadrants. Field loss can occur with normal acuity.

    5. Fluorescein

    Do this after acuity. Use cobalt blue light to look for abrasions, dendritic ulcers, or fluid leaking from the eye (Seidel test) in a suspected penetrating injury.

    6. Fundoscopy / slit lamp

    Check the red reflex, the optic disc and the retina, and use a slit lamp if you are trained. Dilating drops only after acuity and pupils are recorded, and not without senior or ophthalmology advice in a suspected neurological problem.

    Red flags: escalate or discuss with ophthalmology urgently, following local pathways

    • Sudden loss of vision, painful or painless
    • Chemical injury (irrigate first)
    • Suspected penetrating injury or ruptured globe
    • A new RAPD, or a new irregular or fixed pupil
    • Severe eye pain with headache, vomiting or haloes (possible acute angle closure)
    • Visual symptoms with features of giant cell arteritis
    • Contact lens wearer with a painful red eye
    • Swollen, tense orbit after trauma with reduced vision (possible retrobulbar haemorrhage)
    • New double vision with a painful third nerve palsy
    • Very poor vision (e.g. CF or worse) that is new or unexplained
